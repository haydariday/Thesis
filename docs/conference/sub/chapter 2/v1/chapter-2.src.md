# CHAPTER 2 LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Automated Game Playtesting

Playtesting lets players try a game under development so that designers can see how the game is actually experienced. It reveals faults in the game as well as problems in its design, such as levels that turn out harder than intended [@weber2020]. However, manual playtesting depends on many human testers over many sessions, which makes it resource-intensive to repeat after every design change [@prasetya2026; @zhao2025]. Automated playtesting reduces this cost by letting software agents play the game while recording what happens.

The earliest automated playtesters are hand-crafted. Prasetya et al. [@prasetya2026] use the APLib framework to build highly controlled test agents through goal structures and domain-specific languages. However, building such agents requires significant programming effort with a steep learning curve. Francillette et al. [@francillette2025] propose a mathematical model that evaluates the difficulty of platformer games automatically. Their model is deterministic and needs no sophisticated AI, yet it is limited to the platformer genre while demanding a complex setup.

Learning-based playtesters remove part of this manual work. Gudmundsson et al. [@gudmundsson2018] train a deep neural network on human moves in a puzzle game, after which it plays new levels to predict their difficulty for players. Such an agent needs a large set of human play data before it can be used. Deep reinforcement learning agents learn from their own play instead, yet they need large training resources. Even when these agents outperform human players, they lack the natural behavioral patterns of human play [@delfosse2025]. Holmgård et al. [@holmgard2019] narrow this gap through procedural personas, which are agents tuned to represent different playstyles. Their personas show that a playtester can cover more than one way of playing, although each persona still relies on heuristics evolved for the specific game.

LLM-based playtesters are the most recent group. LLM agents receive the rules and the current game state as text, so they can play a new game without game-specific training [@xiao2025]. Zhao and Tang [@zhao2025] focus on match-3 games, where they address the inability of an LLM to "see" the game by converting the board into numerical representations. Their playtester reaches strong code coverage while detecting bugs effectively. Bateni and Whitehead [@bateni2024] show that LLM agents in Slay the Spire can plan over a long horizon without specialized training, which lets them adapt to changes in the game rules. Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025] go further by using LLM agents to measure the difficulty of Wordle and Slay the Spire. Their agents play worse than humans, yet the difficulty patterns the agents experience resemble those of human players. However, a single LLM loses too often in most games, so the authors need a handicap system to prevent this. These studies make LLMs a promising option for indie game developers, who rarely have the data or budget that learning-based methods need.

## 2.2 Game Difficulty and Behavioral Performance Measures

Difficulty is closely linked to whether a game is fun. Players may feel a sense of accomplishment when they overcome challenges, particularly when those challenges are significant [@karavidas2022]. For example, Dark Souls was regarded as one of the hardest games of its time, yet the higher the risk a player takes, the greater the reward [@guzsvinecz2024]. Its difficulty drew strong player engagement, which later gave rise to a new sub-genre known as "souls-like" [@klepek2017]. That is why measuring difficulty for the intended player is an important part of game design.

According to Constant et al. [@constant2017], difficulty can be viewed from two sides. Subjective difficulty is how challenging players perceive the game to be, which is hard to measure directly because it is tied to personal skill, stress and emotion. Objective difficulty is the measurable part of difficulty that comes from how players actually perform, independent of their perception. This research is concerned with objective difficulty only, because an automated playtester produces play outcomes rather than perceptions of the game.

Objective difficulty is measured through behavioral performance measures, which are records of what players do and achieve while playing. Typical measures include whether a challenge is completed, how many attempts it takes and how long it takes. Dynamic difficulty adjustment systems use the same kind of measures to judge the skill of a player during play [@weber2020; @romero2023]. Playtesting studies use them to compare agents with humans, such as the level success rate of an agent against the pass rate of human players [@gudmundsson2018].

In Wordle, the natural performance measure is the number of guesses a player needs to find the answer. Fewer guesses indicate an easier puzzle, while more guesses or a lost game indicate a harder one. Engaging Data [@engagingdata] publishes the guess distribution of real players for each past puzzle, from which the average number of guesses per puzzle can be computed. For example, a puzzle that players solve in 3.2 guesses on average is objectively easier than one that takes 5.8 guesses. However, the dataset does not provide further details such as the demographics of the players. This average serves as the human performance baseline in this research, as it does in the Wordle comparison of Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025].

Comparing a playtester with humans raises two separate questions. The first is whether the playtester gives similar outcomes when it plays the same puzzle again, since a single run of an LLM agent may not represent its typical play. The variation of outcomes across repeated runs, such as their standard deviation, shows how reliable the playtester is. The second question is whether its outcomes rank and group puzzles the same way human results do. Agreement can be checked on continuous scores, such as the correlation between average guess counts, or on ordered categories that range from easy to hard. Since the human baselines describe the player population rather than individual players, these comparisons are made per puzzle over a random sample of puzzles.

## 2.3 Related Work

LLM agents have been shown to play a wide range of games. ChessGPT combines policy learning with language modeling for chess [@feng2023], while PokeLLMon reaches human-parity performance in Pokémon battles [@hu2024]. Voyager acts as an embodied agent in Minecraft that keeps learning new skills as it explores [@wang2023]. These agents aim to play well rather than to play like humans, which is a different goal from playtesting.

For playtesting, the key question is whether the results of an agent reflect human players. The LLM playtesters reviewed above play alone with a single playstyle [@zhao2025; @xiao2025], so the variety of human play is not represented. Sreedhar and Chilton [@sreedhar2025] show in a strategic game that multi-agent systems replicate human behavior more closely than single LLMs do. Before LLMs, Holmgård et al. [@holmgard2019] already represented different players through personas with different playstyles. More recently, MIMIC [@chen2025] gives an LLM game tester different personality traits, which leads to more diverse solutions with higher test coverage. Thus, a playtester that captures this variety needs to move beyond a single LLM agent.

Multi-agent debate is one way to combine several LLM agents into one decision. In a debate, agents propose answers before reading the reasoning of the others, after which they revise their answers over several rounds until they agree [@du2024]. Du et al. [@du2024] show that this process improves factual accuracy and reasoning compared with a single agent. Liang et al. [@liang2024] let agents express arguments in a state of "tit for tat", where each agent responds directly to the position of the previous one. A managing agent oversees this exchange to reach the final decision. ChatEval [@chan2024] gives agents different roles in a debate for evaluating text, while finding that the diversity of these roles is important for its results. However, Smit et al. [@smit2024] find that debate does not reliably outperform other prompting strategies, as its results depend strongly on how the debate is set up. Personas also do not always hold in a debate, since agents may conform to their peers or drift away from their assigned views [@baltaji2024].

This research follows the debate structure of Liang et al. [@liang2024] for two reasons. First, its managing agent gives a single point where the arguments end in one action, which a playtester needs because every turn must produce a playable move. Second, agents that argue from different positions keep the debate from collapsing into immediate agreement, which matches the role of playstyle personas [@chan2024]. Since its benefit depends on the setup [@smit2024], this research evaluates the resulting playtester directly rather than assuming that debate improves it.

## 2.4 Research Gap and Positioning

The reviewed work shows a trade-off between generality and human likeness. Hand-crafted playtesters are precise but must be rebuilt for each game [@prasetya2026; @francillette2025]. Learning-based playtesters need either large sets of human play data or large training resources [@gudmundsson2018; @delfosse2025]. LLM playtesters can play a new game from its rules alone, while the difficulty they experience can follow that of human players [@xiao2025]. However, existing LLM playtesters use a single agent with one playstyle, even though personas have long been used to represent different players [@holmgard2019].

Multi-agent debate could reconcile these different playstyles into one consensus action, yet automated consensus frameworks remain absent from current difficulty measurement methods. Three questions therefore remain open. First, LLM outputs vary between calls, so it is unclear whether a playtester built from several debating agents gives consistent outcomes across repeated runs. Even Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025] play each Wordle puzzle 20 times yet report only the average outcome. Second, it is unclear whether the difficulty found by such a playtester still agrees with human performance baselines. Third, it is unclear whether the personas keep their playstyles during the debate, since debating agents may conform to their peers [@baltaji2024]. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has applied persona-based multi-agent debate to measure game difficulty.

This research is positioned where these lines of work meet. It takes playstyle personas from persona-based playtesting [@holmgard2019] while taking the consensus process from multi-agent debate [@liang2024]. Both are then applied to the LLM difficulty measurement of Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025], using Wordle and its public human results [@engagingdata]. Unlike studies that compare multi-agent systems with a single agent [@sreedhar2025; @smit2024], this research validates the playtester as a system. It does so through the consistency of its outcomes (RQ1), their agreement with human difficulty (RQ2) and the behavior of its personas during the debate (RQ3).
