# CHAPTER 2 LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Automated Game Playtesting

Playtesting is conducted to observe how a game under development is experienced by its players. Through playtesting, designers can find faults in the game as well as design problems, such as levels that are harder than intended [@weber2020]. However, manual playtesting is a resource-intensive and rigorous process, since it needs many human testers over many sessions after every design change [@prasetya2026; @zhao2025]. Automatic playtesting addresses this cost by letting software agents play the game while recording each playthrough.

Various methods have been explored for automatic playtesting, ranging from hand-crafted agents to deep learning. Prasetya et al. [@prasetya2026] demonstrate a more "classical" yet highly powerful approach with the APLib framework. Through goal structures and domain-specific languages, they can create highly controlled test agents, although this requires significant programming effort with a steep learning curve. The mathematical model of Francillette et al. [@francillette2025] for platformer games is also noteworthy. This model is highly deterministic and requires no sophisticated AI, yet it is limited to the platformer genre while demanding a complex setup.

Learning-based approaches reduce part of this manual work. Gudmundsson et al. [@gudmundsson2018] train a deep neural network on human moves in a puzzle game, after which the network predicts how hard new levels are for players. However, such an agent can only be used once a large set of human play data is available. Deep reinforcement learning agents learn from their own play instead, yet they demand substantial training resources. While these agents may outperform human players, they lack the natural behavioral patterns of actual human gameplay [@delfosse2025]. Holmgård et al. [@holmgard2019] narrow this gap with procedural personas, which are agents tuned to represent different playstyles. Their personas show that one playtester can cover several ways of playing, although each persona still relies on heuristics evolved for the specific game.

The emergence of LLMs provides an alternative approach for playtesting. LLM agents receive the game rules and the current game state as text, so they can play a new game without game-specific training [@xiao2025]. Zhao and Tang [@zhao2025] adopt this approach for match-3 games. They successfully address the inability of an LLM to "see" the game by converting the game board into numerical representations. As a result, their playtester achieves strong code coverage with effective bug detection. Bateni and Whitehead [@bateni2024] show that LLM agents in Slay the Spire exhibit long-term planning without specialized training, which lets them adapt to changes in the game rules. Within difficulty measurement, Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025] demonstrate that LLMs can indeed be employed to measure the difficulty of Wordle and Slay the Spire. Although their gameplay is inferior to that of human players, the difficulty patterns experienced by the LLMs resemble those perceived by humans. Nevertheless, a single LLM loses too often in most games, which forces the authors to add a handicap system. Thus, LLMs offer a promising option for indie game developers, who rarely have the data or budget that learning-based approaches require.

## 2.2 Game Difficulty and Behavioral Performance Measures

A "fun" game can be linked to the design of its difficulty. Players may feel a sense of accomplishment when overcoming challenges, particularly when those challenges are significant [@karavidas2022]. For example, Dark Souls was dubbed the hardest game of its time, yet the higher the risk the player must take, the greater the reward [@guzsvinecz2024]. The game garnered significant player engagement, which later gave rise to a new sub-genre known as "souls-like" [@klepek2017]. Given the importance of difficulty, measuring it for the intended player is a key part of game design.

According to Constant et al. [@constant2017], there are two types of difficulty. First, subjective difficulty is how challenging players perceive the game to be, which cannot be measured explicitly because it is tied to personal skill, stress and emotion. Second, objective difficulty is the quantifiable aspect of a game that determines how challenging it is, independent of player perception. This research focuses on objective difficulty only, since an automated playtester produces play outcomes rather than perceptions of the game.

Objective difficulty is measured through behavioral performance measures, which record what players do and achieve while playing. Such measures include whether a challenge is completed, how many attempts it takes and how long it takes. Dynamic difficulty adjustment systems rely on these measures to estimate the skill of a player during play [@weber2020; @romero2023]. Playtesting studies use them to compare agents with humans, for instance the level success rate of an agent against the pass rate of human players [@gudmundsson2018].

In Wordle, the difficulty is operationalized through the guess count, which is how many attempts a player requires to identify the target word. Lower guess counts indicate easier words, while higher counts or a lost game indicate harder ones. Engaging Data^[Engaging Data, "Wordle Guess Distribution," <https://engaging-data.com/wordle-guess-distribution/>.] provides the guess distribution of real players for each past puzzle, from which the average number of guesses per puzzle can be computed. For example, a word with an average of 3.2 guesses is objectively easier than one with 5.8 guesses. However, the dataset does not provide additional details such as demographic information about the players. This average serves as the human performance baseline in this research, as it does in the Wordle comparison of Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025].

Comparing a playtester with human players raises two separate questions. The first is whether the playtester gives similar outcomes on the same puzzle again, since a single run of an LLM agent may not represent its typical play. This reliability can be assessed through outcome variance, such as the standard deviation of repeated playthroughs. The second question is whether the playtester ranks and groups puzzles in the same way as human results do. Agreement can be checked on continuous scores, such as the correlation between average guess counts, or on ordered categories from easy to hard. Since the human baselines describe the player population rather than individual players, both comparisons are made per puzzle over a random sample of puzzles.

## 2.3 Related Work

Recent studies demonstrate that LLM agents can play diverse games. ChessGPT bridges policy learning and language modeling for chess [@feng2023], while PokeLLMon reaches human-parity performance in Pokémon battles [@hu2024]. Voyager acts as an embodied agent in Minecraft that keeps learning new skills as it explores [@wang2023]. However, these agents aim to play well rather than to play like humans, which differs from the goal of playtesting.

For playtesting, the main concern is whether the results of an agent reflect human players. The LLM playtesters reviewed above play alone with a single playstyle [@zhao2025; @xiao2025], so they do not account for the diversity of player playstyles. Sreedhar and Chilton [@sreedhar2025] show in a strategic game that multi-agent systems replicate human behavior more closely than single LLMs do. Before LLMs, Holmgård et al. [@holmgard2019] already represented different players through procedural personas. More recently, MIMIC [@chen2025] equips an LLM game tester with different personality traits, which leads to more diverse solutions with higher test coverage. Thus, capturing the diversity of players requires moving beyond a single LLM agent.

Multi-agent debate is one way to combine several LLM agents into a single decision. In a debate, agents propose their own answers before reading the reasoning of the others, after which they revise their answers over several rounds until they agree [@du2024]. Du et al. [@du2024] show that this process improves factual accuracy and reasoning compared with a single agent. Liang et al. [@liang2024] let agents express arguments in a state of "tit for tat", where each agent directly responds to the position of the previous agent. Meanwhile, a managing agent oversees the exchange to obtain the final decision. ChatEval [@chan2024] assigns different roles to agents in a debate for evaluating text, while finding that the diversity of these roles is important for its results. However, Smit et al. [@smit2024] find that debate does not reliably outperform other prompting strategies, as its results depend strongly on how the debate is set up. Personas also do not always hold in a debate, since agents may conform to their peers or drift away from their assigned views [@baltaji2024].

We adopt the debate structure of Liang et al. [@liang2024] for two reasons. First, its managing agent provides a single point where the arguments end in one action, which a playtester needs because every turn must produce a playable move. Second, agents that argue from different positions keep the debate from reaching agreement immediately, which matches the role of playstyle personas [@chan2024]. Since the benefit of debate depends on its setup [@smit2024], we evaluate the resulting playtester directly rather than assuming that debate improves it.

## 2.4 Research Gap and Positioning

The reviewed studies reveal a trade-off between generality and human likeness. Hand-crafted playtesters are highly controlled but must be rebuilt for each game [@prasetya2026; @francillette2025]. Learning-based playtesters require either large sets of human play data or substantial training resources [@gudmundsson2018; @delfosse2025]. LLM playtesters can play a new game from its rules alone, while the difficulty they experience can follow that of human players [@xiao2025]. However, existing LLM playtesters rely on a single agent with one playstyle, even though personas have long been used to represent different players [@holmgard2019].

While multi-agent debate could reconcile these heterogeneous playstyles into one consensus action, automated consensus frameworks remain absent from current difficulty measurement methods. Thus, three questions remain open. First, LLM outputs vary between calls, so it is unclear whether a playtester built from several debating agents produces consistent outcomes across repeated runs. Even Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025] play each Wordle puzzle 20 times yet report only the average outcome. Second, it is unclear whether the difficulty found by such a playtester still agrees with human performance baselines. Third, it is unclear whether the personas keep their playstyles during the debate, since debating agents may conform to their peers [@baltaji2024]. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has applied persona-based multi-agent debate to measure game difficulty.

To accommodate this gap, our research is positioned where these lines of work meet. We take playstyle personas from persona-based playtesting [@holmgard2019] while taking the consensus process from multi-agent debate [@liang2024]. Both are then applied to the LLM difficulty measurement of Xiao and Yang [@xiao2025], using Wordle and its public human results. Unlike studies that compare multi-agent systems with a single agent [@sreedhar2025; @smit2024], this research validates the playtester as a system. It does so through the consistency of its outcomes (RQ1), their agreement with human difficulty (RQ2) and the behavior of its personas during the debate (RQ3).
